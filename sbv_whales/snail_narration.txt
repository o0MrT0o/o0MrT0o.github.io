A snail is crawling through the grass. But those pulsing, neon-green shapes where its eyes should be are not eyes at all. They are living sacs full of parasite larvae — and the parasite is trying to get this snail eaten.

The creature is called Leucochloridium paradoxum, a parasitic flatworm with a life cycle that depends on two very different animals: a snail and a bird.

It begins when a snail eats material contaminated with the parasite’s eggs. Inside the snail, the parasite develops and multiplies. Eventually, it sends swollen brood sacs into the snail’s eyestalks. The sacs have bright bands and pulse like tiny caterpillars.

That is the horrifying part you can see. But the strategy goes further.

A bird that eats the infected snail can become the parasite’s final host. Inside the bird’s digestive system, the parasite matures and reproduces. Its eggs leave with the bird’s droppings, which can later be eaten by another snail. The cycle starts again.

So the parasite needs a bird — but it is currently inside a snail. How does it bridge that gap?

The flashing, caterpillar-like sacs are thought to make the snail’s eyestalks more noticeable and attractive to birds. Researchers have also reported changes in the snail’s behaviour: infected snails can move into more exposed places, where a bird is more likely to spot them.

There is an important scientific caveat. The overall life cycle is documented, and the parasite’s sacs really do pulsate. But popular descriptions often say it completely “controls the snail’s mind.” That is too strong. The exact mechanisms behind the behavioural changes are not fully understood.

And the snail is not a disposable puppet. The parasite’s success depends on a chain of unlikely events: an egg must reach a snail, the parasite must develop, an infected snail must be eaten by a suitable bird, and the next generation must eventually reach another snail.

The green, throbbing eyestalks are not just a bizarre visual. They are part of a transmission strategy that links two hosts and exploits what a predator notices.

One animal becomes the nursery. Another becomes the destination. And the signal that connects them looks like a tiny, wriggling caterpillar.

Nature did not need science fiction to invent a zombie snail. It only needed a parasite with a very specific route through the food chain.

Strange, but verified.
